1calm‑downs

For grown‑ups

This is not a rage room. "Venting" anger is one of the most reliably disproven ideas in psychology — hitting something while stewing about who upset you tends to leave people angrier and more aggressive than doing nothing at all.

So the stomping here is only bait. It's short, it's timed, it can't be replayed on its own, nothing resembling a person is ever a target, and there is deliberately no score for smashing. Then Bunny gets tired and the real part starts: naming the feeling, paced breathing, muscle release, and picking one next move.

The best thing you can do is do it with them. Kids borrow a calm nervous system before they build their own. Sit down, breathe along with the ring, be boring and steady. Afterwards, don't debrief the meltdown — just follow through on whatever they picked on the last screen.

Everything stays on this device. No accounts, no ads, no tracking, nothing sent anywhere. If a child's anger is hurting them or others, happening most days, or not improving, that's worth a conversation with your pediatrician — approaches like PCIT, parent management training, and child CBT have strong evidence behind them, and a game is not one of them.

Grown-up settings

If your child is sensitive to sound, leave the voice off — the pictures carry the whole thing on their own. A voice they didn't expect can make a meltdown bigger, and your own voice regulates them better than any device does.
